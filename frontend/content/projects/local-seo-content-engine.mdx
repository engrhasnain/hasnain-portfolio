## The problem

A local service business needs the right set of pages: one for each service it really offers, and pages for the towns it really serves. Owners describe their area loosely: "about 45 minutes out", "not across the river", "the whole county". Turning that into a list of towns by hand was slow and different every time. The homepage town matters most. A tiny hamlet cannot carry a homepage, but a small resort town that people search for all the time can, and population by itself does not tell them apart. Get the pages wrong and the site misses searches people make, or ends up with several pages chasing the same keyword so none of them rank well. Regional wording trips people up too: the same service often has a different name from one area to the next.

## What I built

- **A footprint from real drive times.** Addresses and towns are geocoded with Google and matched to Google Ads geo targets, refreshed every quarter from the Google Ads API. For each business location, the Routes API measures traffic-aware drive times to every ZIP in range, then exclusions, always-include areas and territory limits, such as a franchise map, are applied on top. Nationwide plans preview how many city pages, states and media markets they cover.
- **Population and demand on every town.** Population comes from a 3-tier cache: the platform's own cache first, then city research already done, then the US Census ACS survey. Google Ads reach and Keyword Planner search volumes are stored with each town, and a new client's top 10 cities are the selected towns with the highest reach. A FastAPI service builds one research record per city from Census ACS, NOAA climate data, OpenStreetMap, Wikipedia and Tavily.
- **A keyword pipeline bounded by an approved service map.** NestJS modules send the proposed service list to the client by text or email and record the answer. From that map, an LLM simplifies the seed terms, DataForSEO returns keywords and questions, Cohere clusters them, and clusters with overlapping search results are merged because they share an intent.
- **One owner for every keyword.** Each cluster goes to a service page, a blog topic, an FAQ, an H2 section on an existing page, or it is filtered out. When two pages compete for the same terms, a cannibalization detector recommends a 301 redirect instead of leaving both pages to split the rankings.
- **A navigation builder.** Services are scored on client priority, search volume and how rankable they are, weighted 40, 35 and 25, with a boost for services that already rank near the top in Search Console.
- **Regional wording checks.** The terms a page uses are checked in three layers: competitor search results, Search Console and People Also Ask, and the language customers use in their reviews.
- **A landing-page system.** New pages pass an eligibility gate and a near-duplicate gate. An approval dial moves from full review to sample review to exceptions only as a client's batches come back clean, but the owner always approves the wording. It also records how pages appear in Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode.

Geography comes first. Drive times use one standard for every client: the next weekday at 10:00 local time at the business, with the zone taken from the Time Zone API. The homepage rule has clear lines. A town under 15,000 people cannot carry the homepage on its own, unless it gets at least 5,000 monthly searches and at least 8 times its own population in searches. At 40,000 people or more, the town is a confident target. Below the line, the site steps up: first to a named region, then to a searched county-level area, then to "Greater X area" for the nearest well-reached city within 40 miles, then to the county. When the owner names a target, the owner's choice wins.

The content side starts from the approved service map, so research never drifts into services the business does not offer. Keywords are gathered, grouped and merged by intent, then sent to the one place on the site that should own them. The navigation and the landing pages are built from that routing, and the cannibalization detector keeps checking that pages stay apart as the site grows.

## Built to be trusted

- **A person settles the doubtful cases.** Geography changes captured on a call, such as adding a city or removing a county, become pending rows, and only an explicit approve click writes them. Each regional term gets a confidence level, and when the three layers disagree, the term goes to a person instead of the system picking one.
- **Read-back, not trust.** After a geography save, the states, counties and cities actually stored are counted and compared with what the write should have left. Any difference marks the save as failed and lists each problem. Switching a page off is checked too: the system re-fetches each page to confirm it has stopped serving.
- **Approvals that cannot drift.** The last approved version of the service list is saved in the same transaction as the approval. A partial "yes" is parsed without guessing, and LLM suggestions only propose values. They never write approved fields.
- **Pruning that only flags.** Pages with no impressions are flagged for merging into the nearest suitable page. Nothing is deleted automatically.
- **A cost ceiling.** Each run is checked against a ceiling before it starts, and again as real costs come in.

- 122 API routes in the Python backend: keyword 33, navigation 30, landing pages 43, topical map 6, map approval 5, linking 3, city research 2, with 17 ORM models behind them.
- 40 API routes across 3 controllers for service area geography: 35 admin, 3 for service area intake, 2 for the client portal, with 10 data models and 14 test files.
- 78 routes and 14 entities across 4 NestJS modules for service taxonomy approval.
- Up to 625 ZIPs measured in one Routes request.
