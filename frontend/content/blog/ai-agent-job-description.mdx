When a new person joins your company, nobody hands them the keys to everything on day one. They get a role, a manager and access to the tools that role needs. Then the access grows as trust grows.

AI agents are joining companies too. Most businesses are giving them access the old way: one shared API key, full permissions, and hope.

## AI agents now get their own identity

At its Gemini at Work event in October 2026, Google introduced coworker agents for Google Workspace. Each one gets its own Workspace account, with an email address, calendar, Drive and a place in the company directory. Google says every action the agent takes is written to an audit trail and attributed to the agent rather than to a person, and that each agent gets role-based permissions approved by the company's security admins. ([Google's announcement](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/welcome-to-gemini-at-work-2026), with independent coverage from [Reworked](https://www.reworked.co/collaboration-productivity/google-debuts-its-all-purpose-gemini-agent-for-work/) and [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/380545/google-gemini-coworker-own-email-address).)

Microsoft took the same road with [Microsoft Entra Agent ID](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/agent-id/authorization-agent-id). Agents get their own identities there too, and Microsoft blocks them from the most powerful admin roles, such as Global Administrator, because "agents should operate with least privilege."

That is a big shift. Until now, an AI assistant mostly worked through a person's account. Now the agent is a coworker with its own seat. And like any new coworker, it needs a job description.

## What is an AI agent job description?

It is the AI version of the role document a new hire gets. On one page, in plain words, it says what the agent is for, which systems and data it can reach, what it may do on its own, what needs a person's approval, what it must never do, who owns it, where its actions are logged and when its access is checked again.

It is not a prompt. A prompt tells the agent how to behave. A job description decides what the agent is *allowed* to do, and the permissions are built from it. So even if the agent is tricked or simply wrong, it can't do more than the page says.

## Why do AI agent permissions matter?

An agent with its own identity is easier to see. It is not automatically safer. Someone still has to decide three things:

1. **What it can read.** Customer records? Invoices? Only this one inbox?
2. **What it can do.** Draft only, or also send, publish, delete and pay?
3. **Who owns it.** When it gets something wrong at 2 AM, whose phone rings?

If nobody writes these down, the answers are "everything", "everything" and "nobody". That is the real risk with agents. It's rarely the model. It's the access around it. OWASP, the security community behind the best-known list of risks for AI applications, has a name for it: [excessive agency](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm062025-excessive-agency/). It means an AI with too many tools, too many permissions, or too much freedom to act without a person. OWASP's newer [Top 10 for Agentic Applications](https://genai.owasp.org/resource/owasp-top-10-for-agentic-applications-for-2026/), published in December 2025, puts identity and privilege abuse on the list as well.

There is a business cost too. Gartner predicts that [over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027), because of rising costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls. In May 2026 it went further: agent failures are most likely when a company doesn't separate what an agent can *do* from what it can *reach*, and Gartner expects 40% of enterprises to [demote or decommission autonomous agents by 2027](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-26-gartner-says-applying-uniform-governance-across-ai-agents-will-lead-to-enterprise-ai-agent-failure) because of governance gaps they only find after an incident in production. A job description is how you find those gaps before launch.

## How do you set permissions for an AI agent?

Before I connect an agent to a real system, I write it a one-page job description. Then I build the access to match it, not the other way around.

![Read, write and destructive actions side by side: reading runs on its own, writing is drafted by the agent and approved by a person, and destructive actions never run without a person](/images/blog/ai-agent-job-description/levels.jpg)

1. **One role, one scope.** Each agent gets the smallest set of tools its job needs. Security teams call this [least privilege](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/least_privilege): every user or process gets only the access its task requires. A content agent can draft a page. It can't touch billing. Access is granted per system with scoped OAuth (the method the [MCP specification](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/latest/basic/authorization) uses for authorization), not one master key.
2. **Read, write and destructive are different things.** I label every tool by what it can change. Reading a record is low risk. Sending an email or publishing a page is higher. Deleting or paying is flagged as destructive and needs a person to approve it.
3. **A human approves anything public.** Pages, posts and review replies wait in an approval queue. The agent does the work, and a person clicks Approve. The [MCP tools specification](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/latest/server/tools) says the same: there should always be a human in the loop who can deny a tool call.
4. **Every action is logged to the agent.** Who asked, which tool ran, what went in, what came out, and what it cost. When something looks wrong, you can trace it in minutes. The MCP specification asks clients to log tool usage for audit, and Google now does it by default for its coworker agents.
5. **An owner and a review date.** Every agent has a named owner. Its access is reviewed like any employee's, and removed when the job is done.

<Callout>

**From a real build:** I built the MCP layer of an [AI operations platform](/projects/ai-operations-platform) for a digital marketing agency: 6 servers, 17 MCPs and over 1,000 tools for Claude. With that many tools, access design took more time than features. Tool names are prefixed by system so the model never confuses two of them, which is also what the MCP specification recommends when tools from several servers meet. Tools load on demand, so an agent only sees what its task needs. Destructive actions are flagged, and anything that goes public waits for a person to approve it.

</Callout>

## The AI agent job description template

This is the one-page version I start from. The image shows it filled in for a content agent, and the list below fills it in for an agent that drafts replies to Google reviews. Copy it for your first agent.

![An example agent job description for a content agent: role, systems it can and can't reach, what it can read, what it can do alone, what needs approval, what it must never do, its owner, its logs and its review date](/images/blog/ai-agent-job-description/template.jpg)

- **Role:** what this agent is responsible for, in one sentence. *Example: drafts replies to new Google reviews for our two branches.*
- **Systems:** which systems it can reach, and which it can't. *Example: Google Business Profile reviews only. Not the CRM, not billing.*
- **Can read:** the data it needs, nothing more. *Example: the review text, star rating and branch name.*
- **Can do without approval:** low-risk actions such as drafting and tagging. *Example: draft a reply and tag the review by topic.*
- **Needs approval:** sending, publishing, changing records. *Example: posting any reply.*
- **Never:** deleting, paying, changing permissions. *Example: deleting or reporting reviews, or offering refunds and discounts.*
- **Owner:** one named person. *Example: the operations manager.*
- **Logs:** where every action is recorded. *Example: every draft and every posted reply, with who approved it.*
- **Review:** when its access is checked again. *Example: every 90 days, or when its tools change.*

If you can't fill in a line, the agent isn't ready for production.

## When don't you need an agent job description?

Not every AI tool is an agent. A chatbot that only answers questions from your documents can't change anything, so it needs [good retrieval](/blog/rag-chatbots-that-dont-make-things-up) more than a job description. A single n8n workflow with one fixed step is often fine as it is. The job description matters once the AI can act: send, publish, book, update or pay.

## Key takeaways

- Google and Microsoft now give AI agents their own identities. That makes agents visible, not safe.
- Most agent risk is access, not the model. OWASP calls it excessive agency.
- Write a one-page job description before the agent gets any access, then build the permissions from it.
- Separate read, write and destructive tools. Keep a person in the loop for anything public or anything that can't be undone.
- Give every agent a named owner, a log and a review date.
