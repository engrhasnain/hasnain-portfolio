Say "Mardan" to a voice assistant and there's a good chance it writes "Martin."

The rest of the sentence comes out fine. The grammar is right, the meaning is clear. Only one word is wrong, and it's the one the whole request depends on: the city, the customer's name, the product they want.

If you're building a voice agent for bookings, orders or support, this is the bug that quietly breaks it.

## Why does speech recognition get names wrong?

Speech models learn from huge amounts of audio. Common words show up millions of times. A surname from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, a Riyadh street or a new brand name may show up a few times, or never.

A [Speech Technology article on the proper noun problem](https://www.speechtechmag.com/Articles/Editorial/Industry-Voices/The-Proper-Noun-Problem-in-Speech-Recognition-176481.aspx) (September 2026) gives three reasons this happens:

1. **Rarity.** Names are rare in training data, so the model has little to go on.
2. **Sound-alikes win.** When the audio could match a familiar word, the model has every statistical reason to pick the familiar one. "Mardan" becomes "Martin."
3. **Tokens.** Unfamiliar names get split into odd pieces instead of being treated as one word.

## Why doesn't your accuracy score show it?

Most teams judge speech AI by word error rate (WER): the share of words it gets wrong. The problem is that WER averages across every word. A transcript with one wrong name in twenty words still looks 95% right.

A paper at LREC 2026 measured this directly. It scored specialist words and everyday words separately. On the hardest test sets, the Whisper models it tested had an [error rate of 0.88 to 0.90 on specialist words, against 0.06 to 0.19 on everyday words](https://aclanthology.org/2026.lrec-1.32/). In plain terms: nearly every specialist word wrong, while the rest of the sentence looked fine. One overall score would hide that gap completely.

For a voice agent, those are not equal errors. A wrong "the" changes nothing. A wrong city sends a driver to the wrong place.

## Why is the gap bigger for languages like Pashto and Urdu?

Languages like Pashto and Urdu have far less public speech data than English. Pashto on Mozilla Common Voice grew from 1.49 hours in mid-2023 to about 2,769 hours in 2025, with about 976 hours validated for training ([Jahani, Dawodi and Baktash, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14062)). That is real progress, and it came from volunteers. But the same paper found the data skews toward young adults, a small group of contributors does most of the recording, and about 42% of clips have no gender label.

So the gap isn't only hours. It's whose voices are in those hours. That's why speech data companies such as Appen, Shaip and LXT now [recruit speakers to record to a script](https://www.troveo.ai/resources/speech-voice-training-data) in dozens of languages. The model isn't short of grammar. It's short of people saying these exact words.

## How do you fix name errors in a voice agent?

These are the five fixes I'd use, in this order.

### 1. Build a test set of your own names first

Before you compare providers, record 50 to 100 real phrases from your use case: your cities, customer names, product names. Say them the way your users do, with their accents and background noise. Then measure how many names come out right, separately from overall accuracy.

This one step tells you more than any public benchmark.

### 2. Give the model the names upfront

Most speech APIs let you pass a list of words to favour:

- **Deepgram** calls it [Keyterm Prompting](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/keyterm). It works on its Nova-3 and Flux models, allows up to 500 tokens of terms per request, and the docs recommend focusing on the 20 to 50 most important ones.
- **Google Cloud Speech-to-Text** calls it [model adaptation](https://docs.cloud.google.com/speech-to-text/docs/v1/adaptation), with phrase sets and custom classes for lists like city or product names.

For a booking agent, that list can come straight from your database: today's customers, your branch cities, your product catalogue.

Research backs this up. Meta researchers gave a language model a list of likely names while it re-checked the speech model's best guesses, and errors on their test set dropped by 16.6%. When the correct names were taken out of the list, the gain fell to 2.7% ([Sun et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00723)). The LREC 2026 paper found the same thing: giving the model the right specialist words in its prompt brought the error rate on those words down by 0.50 to 0.70. The list is what does the work, so it has to contain the right names.

### 3. Correct the transcript against what you know

After transcription, match each name against your real records by sound, not just spelling. If the transcript says "Martin" and your list of service cities has "Mardan," that's almost certainly the answer. A simple phonetic match catches a lot of what the speech model missed. If your agent reaches your records through an [MCP server](/blog/what-is-an-mcp-server), this check can be one more read-only tool.

### 4. Confirm the word that matters

For anything with a consequence, the agent should repeat the key detail back: "Mardan, in KP, right?" One extra second costs far less than a wrong booking. Save it for the important words, so the call doesn't feel like a quiz. It's the voice version of a chatbot [saying "I don't know" instead of guessing](/blog/rag-chatbots-that-dont-make-things-up).

### 5. Fine-tune when the volume justifies it

If you handle thousands of calls in one language and region, fine-tuning on recordings of local speakers saying local names is the long-term fix. It costs more, and you need clean, consented data. But it's the only step that improves the model itself, not just the layers around it.

## When don't you need all of this?

If your voice feature only takes notes in a meeting, a wrong name is annoying but cheap to fix later. Steps 1 and 2 are enough.

The full stack of fixes matters when the agent acts on what it hears: booking, ordering, routing a call or updating a record. At that point the agent also needs clear limits on what it can do, which I cover in [Your AI Agent Needs a Job Description Before It Gets Access](/blog/ai-agent-job-description).

## Key takeaways

- Speech AI usually gets the sentence right and the name wrong, because names are rare in its training data.
- Word error rate hides it. Measure names separately, on your own recordings.
- Give the speech API a list of the names you expect, pulled from your real data.
- Match the transcript against your records by sound, and confirm anything that can't be undone.
- A bigger model won't fix this on its own. Recordings of people who actually say these words every day will.
