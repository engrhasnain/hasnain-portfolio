Every business that asks me for a chatbot has the same fear: *what if it tells a customer something wrong?* It's the right fear. A chatbot that sounds confident and is wrong is worse than no chatbot at all.

## What is RAG?

**Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** is the standard fix. Before the model answers, you search your own documents for the passages that matter and give them to the model along with the question. The model answers from your content, not from memory.

The idea comes from a 2020 research paper by Lewis and colleagues at Facebook AI Research ([the paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)), and it is now how most business chatbots are built.

## Why do RAG chatbots still make things up?

RAG on its own isn't magic. Retrieval can miss the right passage, and the model can still fill the gap with something that sounds right. Most of the time the cause is boring: messy documents, chunks cut in the wrong place, or nobody checking what retrieval actually found.

## Why does a wrong chatbot answer matter?

A wrong answer from your bot is still your answer. In 2024, a tribunal in British Columbia ordered Air Canada to pay a customer after its website chatbot told him he could claim a bereavement refund after his trip, which the airline's real policy didn't allow. Air Canada argued the chatbot was responsible for its own words. The tribunal called that "a remarkable submission" and said the chatbot "is still just a part of Air Canada's website." ([CBC News](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/british-columbia/air-canada-chatbot-lawsuit-1.7116416))

OWASP, the security community behind the best-known list of risks for AI applications, puts this in its top ten as [misinformation](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm092025-misinformation/): a model produces false information that sounds credible, and people trust it without checking.

The good news is that you can catch most of it before launch.

## How do you stop a RAG chatbot from making things up?

These are the five things I do on every build.

1. **Clean the data before anything else.** Most wrong answers start in the documents, not the model. Old price lists, duplicate policies and PDFs with broken tables all end up in the index if you let them. I remove outdated files, fix the text extraction, and keep one source of truth for each topic.
2. **Chunk by meaning, not by character count.** Splitting every document into fixed 500-character pieces cuts ideas in half. I split on headings and sections where possible, keep a small overlap between chunks, and store the document title and section with each chunk, so the model knows where the text came from. Anthropic's research on [contextual retrieval](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval) backs this up: adding context to each chunk cut failed retrievals by 49% in their tests, and by 67% with a re-ranking step on top.
3. **Check retrieval separately from generation.** If the right passage never reaches the model, no prompt will save the answer. I keep a small set of real questions with known answers and check that retrieval finds the right passages *before* tuning prompts. When retrieval is weak, hybrid search (keywords plus embeddings) or a re-ranking step usually fixes it.
4. **Let the model say "I don't know".** The prompt says clearly: answer only from the provided context, show where the answer came from, and if the answer isn't there, say so and offer a person. Customers forgive "let me connect you with the team". They don't forgive invented refund policies.
5. **Hand off to a human, and learn from it.** Every bot I build has a way out: email, live chat or a [WhatsApp hand-off](/projects/whatsapp-ai-chatbot). The questions it couldn't answer are the most useful data you have. They show exactly which documents to add next.

## How do you test a RAG chatbot before launch?

Before launch, I run every bot against a short list of real questions, in three groups:

- **Questions it should answer**, each paired with the passage the answer must come from. If the bot answers correctly but from the wrong document, that still counts as a miss.
- **Questions it shouldn't answer**, like the price of a product you don't sell. The right reply is "I don't know" and a hand-off, not a guess.
- **Questions written the way customers really write**: typos, two questions in one message, or the wrong product name.

When the bot gets one of these wrong, the fix almost always lives in the data or the retrieval, not in the prompt. That's why I test them in that order.

## What does a RAG chatbot architecture look like?

Here is the whole pipeline in one place, from the documents you already have to an answer in the chat. Each stage is simple on its own, and each one can fail on its own, which is why I check them one at a time.

1. **Load and clean the documents.** The files come in from wherever they live: PDFs, help pages, policies, product sheets. I extract the text and drop anything outdated or duplicated, as in [step 1 above](#how-do-you-stop-a-rag-chatbot-from-making-things-up).
2. **Split them into chunks.** Each document is cut along its headings and sections, and every chunk keeps its document title and section name, as in step 2.
3. **Store embeddings in a vector store.** Each chunk is turned into an embedding, a list of numbers that captures what the text means, and saved in a vector store. For most builds I use PostgreSQL with pgvector.
4. **Retrieve.** When a question comes in, it is turned into an embedding the same way, and the store returns the chunks closest to it in meaning. Hybrid search adds a keyword match, which helps with names, codes and product numbers.
5. **Re-rank.** A second pass scores those chunks against the question more carefully and keeps only the best few. Stages 4 and 5 are what step 3 checks on their own.
6. **Answer only from the retrieved text.** The model gets the question, those passages and a clear instruction: answer from this text alone, and cite the document and section the answer came from.
7. **Say "I don't know" when the text isn't there.** If nothing relevant comes back, or the passages don't answer the question, the bot says so and offers a person, as in steps 4 and 5.

The [launch tests](#how-do-you-test-a-rag-chatbot-before-launch) follow the same order: documents and retrieval first, the prompt in stage 6 last.

## When don't you need RAG?

If your questions have a small set of fixed answers, a good FAQ page or a scripted flow may be enough. If the answer changes every hour, like live stock levels or today's prices, the bot should look it up from your system directly, through an API or an [MCP server](/blog/what-is-an-mcp-server), instead of from documents. And once the bot can take actions instead of only answering, it needs [a job description and limits](/blog/ai-agent-job-description) as well.

RAG is for when the answers live in documents your team already trusts.

## Key takeaways

- RAG makes a chatbot answer from your documents, but it can still make things up if the data or retrieval is weak.
- Your business can be held to what its chatbot says, as Air Canada found out.
- Clean the documents, chunk by meaning and test retrieval with real questions before you touch the prompt.
- Tell the bot to say "I don't know" and hand off to a person when the answer isn't there.
- The questions it can't answer show you which documents to add next.
